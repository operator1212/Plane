1.0

Summary

Programme intent

Serac is a tailless, twin-engine fighter built to hold air superiority across ocean-scale distances and to lead a small group of uncrewed aircraft while it does. The configuration was ranked by three requirements in a fixed order: range, then signature, then agility. Most of what makes the aircraft look the way it does follows from that ranking.

Priority 1Reach

A large, deep planform carries fuel in the wing and centre body. Adaptive-cycle engines trade thrust for economy in cruise. The aircraft is sized to fight at the far end of its radius without a tanker.

Priority 2Quiet

Every planform edge falls on two angles. Inlets sit under the chine, exhausts run into shielded troughs, and nothing is carried outside the skin. The aircraft emits by choice, never by default.

Priority 3Command

The cockpit is designed for directing teammates rather than flying waypoints. One pilot sets intent for up to four uncrewed aircraft and approves what they propose.

What is new here

Articulating tip panels. The outer 1.7 m of each wing folds down about a streamwise hinge. Flat, the panels give span and a clean signature for subsonic cruise. Folded, they act as vertical surfaces and give the tailless airframe the directional stability it needs at supersonic speed. Section 4.0 covers the three positions.

A single continuous edge. The chine that starts at the nose tip becomes the wing leading edge without a break. One line runs from the radome to the wingtip, and the rest of the design is organised around it.

Shielded exhaust deck. Two-dimensional nozzles exit into ceramic-tiled troughs on the upper aft deck, so the hottest metal on the aircraft cannot be seen from below or from the side.

2.0

Design language

Two angles, one edge

The shape is controlled by a very small number of rules. Each one exists for an engineering reason, and together they give the aircraft its visual identity.

Two edge directions. Every major planform edge is swept at 50°, either forward or aft. The starboard leading edge is parallel to the port outer trailing edge and to the inner trailing edge of the notch, and the same holds in mirror image. Radar returns from edges are concentrated into a few narrow directions instead of spread across the sky. Streamwise edges are allowed in exactly two places: the wingtip chords and the tip-panel hinge.

One edge from nose to tip. The forebody chine grows from the nose tip, turns once at the wing junction, and continues as the leading edge. There is no separate strake, no inlet lip interrupting the line from above, and no step at the canopy.

Nothing hangs. Air data comes through flush ports. Antennas are conformal patches. Payload is internal. The only things that break the skin in flight are the canopy and the tip panels, and both are part of the outer mould line.

Proportion follows function. The long forebody puts the cockpit well ahead of the inlets, which gives the pilot a clear view over the nose and leaves room for a large nose array. Most of the volume sits in a deep centre body and thick wing roots, where it serves as fuel.

4.0

Articulating tip panels

The defining feature

A tailless aircraft is quiet and efficient but short of directional stability, and the shortfall grows with Mach number. Serac solves this by moving part of the wing.

The outer panel of each wing rotates downward about a streamwise hinge. The panels are driven together for stability and trim, and differentially for yaw. A split drag rudder on each panel trailing edge gives yaw authority at low speed, when deflecting the panels would cost too much lift.

Folding wingtips at supersonic speed were demonstrated on a large bomber prototype in the 1960s, where the drooped tips also added compression lift under the wing. Serac applies the principle to a much smaller aircraft, and uses modern actuation to make the panels a primary flight control rather than a two-position configuration change.

The hinge line is the most difficult signature detail on the airframe. It is covered by a flexible conductive fairing and aligned with the airflow so that it presents a single streamwise edge when the panels are flat.

0°Flat

Subsonic cruise and loiter. Full span, lowest induced drag, and the cleanest edge alignment.

≈ 30°Transonic

Acceleration through the transonic region. Restores directional stability as the aerodynamic centre moves aft.

≈ 65°Supersonic

Sustained supersonic cruise. The panels act as fins and reduce trim drag. Span drops to about 12.8 m.

5.0

Signature philosophy

Shape first, materials second

Most of the signature budget is spent on shape. Coatings and edge materials are used to finish the job, never to rescue a shape that reflects in the wrong direction.

Inlets. The two inlets are ventrolateral, tucked under the chine and behind the wing junction. From above they are hidden entirely. From the side the chine overhangs the aperture. Each duct curves inboard and upward so the engine face is out of line of sight from any direction.

Exhausts. Two-dimensional nozzles exit onto the upper aft deck. The trough floor extends a short distance behind each nozzle so that the exhaust plume and the hottest structure are screened from below and from the side. The trough is lined with ceramic matrix tiles that take the heat and can be replaced individually.

Doors and joints. Every door edge is either serrated or aligned with one of the two edge families. The radome joint, the nozzle flap edges and the gear doors all use the same 50° teeth. Panels that are opened often use a seam treatment that does not have to be re-applied after each access.

Emissions. The aircraft can fly a full mission without radiating. Passive sensors and teammate data build the picture, and the nose array transmits only when the pilot, or a rule the pilot has set, decides it should.

6.0

Propulsion & heat

The heat budget drives the design

Serac uses two adaptive three-stream turbofans. In cruise the engines route more air around the core for economy. In combat they close that path down for thrust. The third stream is a cool, low-pressure flow that the aircraft uses as a heat sink.

A long-range aircraft with powerful sensors produces a lot of waste heat and has very little skin area through which to reject it without being seen. On Serac, heat was treated as a first-order design constraint alongside weight and drag. Fuel is the primary heat sink. The third stream absorbs what the fuel cannot, and the warmed air leaves through the vents beside each engine hump, where it mixes into the exhaust flow over the aft deck.

Electrical generation is sized for the sensor suite and for growth. The aircraft is designed with margin for power and cooling that later systems will need, because adding either after the airframe is built is very expensive.

7.0

Structure, materials & finish

Fewer parts, fewer seams

The airframe is built from as few large pieces as possible. Each wing upper skin is a single co-cured panel from root to hinge. Fewer skins mean fewer joints, and fewer joints mean fewer places where the signature treatment can fail and fewer hours spent restoring it.

The low-observable finish is applied mostly as factory-made film rather than sprayed in the hangar. Film has consistent thickness and can be replaced in sections. Seams on panels opened every flight use a reusable conductive gasket rather than a filler that must be cured.

The paint is a cool graphite with a faint metallic sheen, chosen for high-altitude sky backgrounds. Low-visibility markings are printed in a darker tone of the same grey. Walkways, rescue markings and tail codes remain on the aircraft but only read at close range.

The canopy is a single transparency with one aft frame. Its metallised coating keeps radar energy out of the cockpit and gives the canopy a bronze cast in daylight.

Sensors & apertures

One picture from many windows

Serac carries no single primary sensor. Radar, passive receivers, infrared and teammate data all feed one fused track picture, and the pilot works with tracks rather than with individual sensors.

  • 1Nose array. Fixed, canted multifunction array behind the radome.
  • 2Chine arrays. Side-looking panels set into the lower chine flanks.
  • 3Distributed aperture windows. Six infrared windows giving spherical coverage to the helmet.
  • 4Chin electro-optical sensor. Faceted window for long-range passive tracking.
  • 5Satellite communications. Conformal patch on the spine.
  • 6Teammate datalink. Directional low-probability-of-intercept patches on each tip panel.
  • 7Lower datalink and identification.
  • 8Wideband receivers in the tip panel leading edges.
  • 9Rearward receivers in the outer trailing edges.
9.0

Cockpit & interface

Designed for intent, not input

One pilot flies the aircraft, fights it and directs four others. The interface is built on the assumption that the pilot's attention is the scarcest resource on board.

No head-up display

The helmet is the primary flight reference and sees through the airframe using the distributed aperture windows. One wide display below the canopy rail carries everything else.

Colour means something

The display is monochrome by default. Amber is reserved for items that need a decision and red for threats and emergencies, so colour always signals a change.

Touch where it is calm, keys where it is not

Touch is used for planning and review. Anything a pilot might need under load has a physical key, a detent or a switch on the side-stick or throttle.

Tally proposes, the pilot decides

The cockpit assistant, Tally, watches fuel, systems and teammates and turns what it sees into short proposals. Nothing that changes a teammate's task, emits energy or releases a weapon happens without the pilot's approval.

10.0

Teaming: Quorum

Crewed lead, uncrewed partners

Serac is designed as the crewed lead of a group of up to four collaborative aircraft. The group is called a quorum. The name reflects the rule that governs it: the uncrewed aircraft plan and act among themselves within limits the pilot sets, and anything outside those limits comes back to the pilot for a decision.

A typical arrangement puts the uncrewed aircraft forward and to the flanks, where they take on the tasks that carry the most exposure. One senses actively, one relays, one gathers electronic signals and one waits in reserve. Serac stays behind and stays quiet, building its picture from what the others report.

The pilot assigns tasks by intent, such as "cover the northern sector" or "hold as relay", and the aircraft work out their own routes. When the datalink degrades, each aircraft falls back to the last intent it received and to a conservative default. It never escalates on its own.

12.0

Configuration lineage

How C-4 was reached
C-1 · Discarded

Tailed delta

Twin canted fins gave good handling, but the fins dominated the side signature and added weight far from the centre of gravity.

C-2 · Discarded

Lambda, dorsal inlet

Very clean from below, but the dorsal inlet starved the engines at high angles of attack. Range was also short of the target.

C-3 · Discarded

Diamond, fixed anhedral tips

Permanently drooped tips fixed the stability problem but cost span and subsonic efficiency on every flight hour.

C-4 · Selected

Diamond, articulating tips

Keeps the full-span wing for cruise and the stability of fins when it matters. Inlets under the chine and troughs on the aft deck.
